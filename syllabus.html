Course Description

Organizations that turn data into good decisions need people who can do more than just run scripts in a given software or use AI to do the job for them. They need analysts who can start from a business problem, choose an appropriate method, evaluate a model honestly, interpret the result carefully, and recommend an action, including what the model cannot do.

This course introduces the core machine learning tools used in modern business analytics, with applications from marketing, finance, operations, and related areas. You will implement methods in R, work from real-world datasets, and practice professional communication through slide decks, recorded presentations, and an individual project presented in class at the end of the course. The course prepares you for consulting and analytics-oriented roles in finance, operations, marketing, and similar fields.

Because generative AI is now part of how technical work gets done, you will also learn how to use it to improve your understanding of the methods and their software implementation, while remaining responsible for verification, judgment, and academic integrity.

Prerequisites: Statistics (70-207 or 36-200 or 36-220 or equivalent). Comfort with R and basic regression is expected. Prior machine learning is not required.

Learning Objectives

On completing the course, you should be able to:

  1. Build analytics models using R and use them for business decision-making.
  2. Translate a data-driven business problem into analytical models based on its purpose, available data, solution methods, evaluation metrics, and business decision-making.
  3. Exercise judgment when choosing among supervised and unsupervised methods, model families, features, validation strategies, and regularization techniques, always letting the problem and the data determine the choice rather than habit.
  4. Communicate the results of technical business data analysis models to a business audience to convince them of its value, validity, effectiveness, and potential limitations.
  5. Learn how to use AI effectively to improve your coding skills and to augment the quality of your work in translating data-driven business problems into analytical models and business decisions.

Course Materials

The main readings will be based on suggested readings from the main textbook, along with my notes. I write those notes from the parts of ISLR that we actually use that week, and I add the pieces ISLR does not cover. I will post class slides PDFs on the course website and Canvas after class.

  • [ISLR] James, Witten, Hastie, and Tibshirani. An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in R (2nd ed.). The book is free online. The notes will point you to the sections we used in class.
  • Additional notes and readings will be posted on the website and Canvas as needed. Check that you can open the materials and let me know if something does not work.
  • We will use R and RStudio (Posit). Labs and homeworks start from scripts I provide: your job is to understand them, interpret the results, and modify them based on the problem at hand. Bring a laptop to every lab.
  • There is a setup page on the website with the packages to install. Please do this before the first lab and email me if something doesn’t work, so we can use lab time for methods rather than troubleshooting installation. Make sure you do this after the first class and reach out if there are issues to solve.

Delivery of Lectures and Labs

Our lectures and labs are in person. They will not be recorded. A typical week looks like this:

  • Monday: Most weeks, a student pair opens with a short presentation introducing the method through a business problem. I then continue with the discussion and the details (formulas, short code, and interpretation).
  • Wednesday: Lab in R. You will get the problem before class and a starter notebook with parts already working. In the hour we work on approach, interpretation, figures, and what the results mean for the decision.

Assessment Descriptions

Homeworks. There will be four individual homeworks. Each one is a new case using the methods we have covered, not a repeat of the lab. I will give you a case, the data, a codebook, and starter code. You submit three pieces on Canvas: a runnable R notebook, at most six slides, and a video of at most five minutes. The slides and the video should tell the same story as the notebook: the problem, the evidence, the decision, and the limit. More details about the expectations will be available on Canvas.

You may study with classmates. The write-up, the code you submit, and the recommendation have to be yours. AI is allowed on homework if you disclose it (see below). You must run the code and be able to explain the important parts of how you reached the decision. If you hide AI use, or you cannot explain the work in the video, or I suspect you used AI inappropriately, I will ask you to present in person. Depending on how you handle the questions in person, the grade on that homework can go as low as 0, and you may be reported for an academic integrity violation.

Quizzes. There will be four short in-class paper quizzes, designed for about 10–15 minutes. They test whether you understand the ideas and can apply the methods to understand and make business decisions. There is no make-up for quizzes.

Pair presentations. Because this is a small class, participation matters. Student pairs will prepare a short presentation (about 8–10 minutes, compact slide deck) at least twice in the semester to introduce that week’s topic. We will then transition into a lecture about the method and the empirical implementation. The rubric will be on the course site / Canvas. The official pairing will be set once we know with certainty how many students are in the course.

The midterm will be in person during class on Wednesday 7 October (75 minutes). Closed notes. No electronic devices. The exam covers weeks 1–5 only: linear regression, metrics, train/test and generalization, regularization, and cross-validation/model selection.

Final project. The final project is an individual project. It’s based on a business problem coming from a real-world dataset. The student will then build models in R to better understand the problem, present a recommendation, and discuss the decisions in an in-class presentation at the end of the semester. I will publish the project brief (including data and problem options once they are ready) by 28 October. I will share exact instructions for what’s expected at checkpoint 1 and during the final presentations later in the semester. Presentations are 30 November and 2 December.

Attendance and participation are part of the grading and will be combined. Please come to each class ready to participate in the discussion of the material or bring questions that could benefit the rest of the group. By mid-semester, I will share your participation and attendance grades, so you have time to adjust appropriately during the second part of the semester, if needed.

How will attendance and participation be graded? If you have various excused or unexcused absences, that counts toward limited participation because you need to be in the classroom to participate. Similarly, if you attend all the classes but rarely participate during class, don’t expect your grade to be solely based on attendance. Finally, if you arrive to class early, but leave the classroom for long periods, don’t expect your grade to be based on arriving on time. I acknowledge that grading participation could be subjective. Still, I will do my best to evaluate students not only on quantity but also on the quality of their participation.

Final grades

Final grades are based on your cumulative score using the weights above. Students with 90–100% receive an A; 80–89% a B; 70–79% a C; 60–69% a D; below 60% an R.

A quick note on calculating your cumulative score: Please use the weights in the table above. I compute the course grade from those weights. Canvas is not always reliable here. If Canvas and that calculation disagree, email me.

In the interests of fairness, we apply the same rules to everyone. Therefore, we cannot modify the cumulative score formula or offer extra work to raise your grade to accommodate your circumstances. The only exception is for midterms missed due to documented illness or a family emergency. Some students will inevitably fall close to, but below, a cutoff when we assign grades. Such students sometimes ask for cutoffs to be reduced. Please do not do this. If you are just below a cutoff, you may be close to the lowest A or B but still some way from the average A or B, and we consider these when assigning cutoffs.

Guidelines for Late or Missed Work

We do not accept late submissions for quizzes or homework. There are two reasons for this. First, we will post or discuss solutions within 3-5 days after the deadline. Grades will be available within 5-10 days after submission. Second, to be fair to everyone, we want to give all students the same amount of time to complete the work (and it is impossible for us to verify whether work was done late or just turned in late).

There is no make-up opportunity for missed quizzes.

We believe these standards create an environment most conducive to learning. This behavior also reflects common business culture. As CMU Qatar Business Administration students, part of your business education is to model the behavior that you will demonstrate in the workplace.

QBA students attend all scheduled classes. Unless I explicitly state that another way of completing the work is acceptable, you must attend. If you cannot attend, contact me in advance when possible, and as soon as possible if not. You are responsible for obtaining notes and materials.

QBA students use electronics only for class purposes. On Mondays, laptops are closed unless I ask you to open them for a short demonstration. On Wednesdays, laptops are required for R. Phones are silent and put away. Do not check email, message, or browse during class.

Use of Generative AI Tools

Certain assignments in this course will permit or even encourage the use of AI tools, such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and others, to improve your coding and understanding. When AI use is allowed, it will be clearly stated in the assignment. Otherwise, AI is not allowed by default.

When AI is allowed, you must acknowledge how you used it. For instance: “I used Gemini to debug an error in my glmnet call and edited the code for correctness.” You are responsible for assessing whether the output is valid. You may not earn full credit if inaccurate or invalid information is found in your work. Misrepresenting AI use is an academic integrity violation. Expectations differ across courses; email me if you are unsure what is allowed on a given assignment.

Two ways to think about the use of AI in this course. Depending on the assignment, AI is accepted with caution or not allowed.

Yellow level: AI with caution

Homework and the project (when the assignment allows it). You may use AI to learn, debug, and clarify concepts. The analysis, interpretation, and recommendation must be yours. You must run the code, understand the results, and make sure the figures in the video match the notebook.

Do Don’t
Disclose how you used AI Hide that you used AI
Verify every number and chart Paste answers you do not understand
Use AI to explain an error you then fix Let AI invent results, metrics, or a business claim
Make the decision yours Outsource the recommendation

Red level: No AI assistance is permitted for this assignment

Quizzes and the midterm. No AI tools for any purpose on these assessments. The oral defense of the project and the in-class Q&A also require that you can explain the work without AI in the room.

Do Don’t
Prepare from notes, labs, and your own practice Use chatbots or AI coding assistants on the assessment
Ask me if you are unsure about the rules Risk an integrity violation
Show your own understanding
